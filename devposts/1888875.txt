Exactly, yes. Consider that when a pixel of the wall is being drawn, there isn't a conveniently searchable data structure describing where the walls are. So the alternatives are either to have such a structure so that rays can be fired into it (this is what RTX is doing) or to divide the world into small patches, and pre-calculate visibility between patches (these are called radiosity techniques). For the ray-based technique, you're limited by how many rays you can fire in a single frame (and you need a lot, since you don't know in advance where the bright parts of the scene are), especially in cases like this where you probably need multiple bounces before the light properly filters into the room. For radiosity techniques, you're limited by how much resolution you can afford on the patch list, and you generally end up with a lower quality lighting result for anything that moves or is dynamically placed.
This isn't to say that there are no improvements we could make to our bounced lighting. I'd love it if we found time in the schedule to allow for larger scale bounces, and that (I expect) would add a lot of natural feel to some scenes, but setups like this I would expect to result in unstable or weird-looking results.
