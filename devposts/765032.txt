I started working in the video game industry as a QA tester which is a great starting position to learn about the industry and how things work. It also gives people a chance to interact with developers and ask to help them out with work for areas they might be interested in helping out with.
After having been in college for 3 years studying zoology and deciding that it wasn't for me, I was kinda lost and going through a period of time where I wasn't sure what I was going to do with my life. I was (unhappily) applying for basically any kind of career I could because I have always been better at learning while doing rather than learning while studying. In a stroke of luck, I was hanging out with some friends at table top game store and they found someone that was working as QA for an SCEA studio. I applied and got a job doing QA testing as a temporary employee on PS3 games that were intended to be launch titles for the platform (Warhawk, Motorstorm, etc). Working as a QA tester for 2 years helped me learn a lot of really interesting stuff about the gaming industry and myself; including that I value a good balance between work and life. Being a temporary employee as a QA tester means long periods of time without work followed by long periods of time filled with 80 hour work weeks. I also learned to push myself out of my comfort zone to ask questions about things. At this point in my life, I was really bad at approaching others but being able to continue working on video games was important enough to me that I forced myself outside of my comfort zone so that I could succeed. After working there for 2 years, they decided not to renew my contract because while I was really good at my job, a lot of my coworkers didn't like me. People skills wasn't one of my strong areas when I was younger and this helped serve as an awakening for me that I needed to work to be more likable to people that I work with.
After that, I got a job doing QA for Sony Online Entertainment where I was a QA tester for an MMO they were making called Free Realms that was aimed at a younger audience. Initially when I stated working there, one of their senior QA testers told me to show him every bug that I wrote for the first two weeks that I was working there. Despite my prior experience having worked in QA, bug formatting rules at SOE were different and much more specific so having his input on the bugs I was writing helped me develop better bug writing habits. It makes a lot of sense for an MMO QA team to have incredibly specific and strict bug writing rules because there end up being so many bugs that without following those specific rules, it gets really difficult to find bugs. Important lesson to take to heart: just because something worked one way at a prior job doesn't meat it will work the same way at a new one. Similarly, just because something worked at a prior job doesn't mean it was right or the best way to do it. In working at SOE, my QA leads found that I had a talent for finding the really weird bugs and over time, I was given more and more responsibility which ended up with me being an assistant QA Specialist for the pet system. It makes sense for an MMO QA team to have their experienced QA testers specialize in different aspects of the game because the game just ends up being far too big for people to efficiently test everything and having senior QA members specialize in different aspects of the game allows the QA leadership to have subject matter experts they can contact in the event that they have a question about a specific system. Things were going really well here for me. I was doing a better job of getting along with my coworkers and I got along with the QA leads. I also made the mistake of asking if it would be a problem if I wanted to make my own game which resulted in me being fired.
Fortunately enough, a few weeks prior to my having been fired, I had secretly flown out to Austin to have an interview to work as a Game Master for Blizzard on World of Warcraft. A few months after having been fired from SOE, they gave me a call and asked "Hey did you still want to work here" to which I think I said something to the affect of "Hell yes". So I took a really big risk and spent $2k to move my life from the west coast to Texas. Like the prior jobs I'd had, this job taught me a lot about the gaming industry from a totally different perspective. There's a lot that goes into supporting the existence of an MMO that I had never even thought of prior to having to work on supporting that exact thing. Example: Did you know that criminal organizations use video games to generate dirty money? They do it by using stolen credit cards to either purchase or pay subscription for game accounts, farm or steal in game money, and then sell that for real money (this is why people should never buy game currency from third party websites. It funds this behavior and basically pays for account compromises). By the time the purchase on the credit card gets charged back and the game account is deactivated, they've already made the real world money they need. In addition to being able to learn way more about how the gaming industry works, it also gave me a perfect environment to learn more about person to person interactions, different styles of communication, and overall how to be less of a jerk when interacting with others. Similar to how my QA leads and Senior QA testers at prior jobs taught me to test games, my Seniors and Managers at Blizzard taught me how to communicate with our players. One of my favorite examples of this is something that my first manager taught me. If someone's angry about something, it's because they care and the secret to turning that into a positive experience for them is to identify what it is they're angry about and make an effort to understand it. My prior QA experience helped me a lot in this job in understanding how games break and being able to both better communicate and better troubleshoot issues players experienced. I also found a way to have fun with the conversations I had with players which helped them enjoy their time talking with me. If you ever got a waffle from a Game Master at the end of a conversation (#), that was me.  Fortunately and unlike my prior two jobs, I managed to not get fired from this one! After working at Blizzard for 5 years, some changes were made to the job that moved me away from the investigatory work that I was really good at so I started looking for jobs elsewhere.
That's how I ended up working at CIG. Justin and Gerard interviewed me for a QA position and I used the experience from my prior gaming industry positions to help establish the QA specialist system for CIG. After working in QA here for a time, the DevOps team snagged me after noticing my problem solving and technical skills. Today, I'm a DevOps engineer and amount to something that is similar to Mission Control for Star Citizen. I'm unique in that I'm the only person in the company that has experience working as QA, Game Support, and a DevOps engineer so I am frequently in touch with all of these departments helping with areas where our work overlaps.
So that's the short answer to how I got to where I am now. With regards to answering your question about tactics:
-A good resume and cover letter are always the first step. Personally I'm pretty bad at interviews unless I practice for them but I'm a decent writer.
-When applying for a job, research the company. Think of things they might ask you, practice your answers in front of a mirror. When Justin and Gerard interviewed me, they said they felt like I was interviewing them.
-The gaming industry isn't as concerned with college degrees as some other industries are but rather, tends to focus more on what a person can do. There's no way to measure how good someone is based on a major so frequently a company will give someone a programming test, ask an artist to show their portfolio or have them do an impromptu art test, ask a potential designer difficult design questions to see if they can come up with creative solutions, etc. For the QA job at SCEA, they basically had us test builds of certain games that were already broken and I found some rather amusing bugs.
-For programming, if you have a company you want to work for, figure out what game engine they are using and try programming something using it (if it's free and open to the public). If not, figure out what language they use for their game(s) as that's probably going to be the language they use for any tests they give out. These are pretty much always listed on the job listings a company will have.
-Participate in a game jam as a programmer. Having made something together with a team speaks volumes. There are also frequently mentors from companies at these events that you can talk to and ask questions.
-In my experience, it's easier to get a job with larger companies in entry level positions that might have higher turnover rate (such as being a temp QA tester). This can be a way to get experience and learn about the industry but these larger companies also tend to have less upward mobility.
-People skills are important regardless of what kind of position you work in. We all have to do customer service at some point in our life but not everyone is taught that our coworkers are what's referred to as an internal customer when they need help from us. This is especially important when working as QA or as an engineer because a lot of the time, we are responsible for telling people that the thing they just spent days working on is broken. It's important to approach these interactions in a way that helps encourage cooperation.
-Ask people questions. If you find someone isn't helpful with the answers they give you, ask someone else until you can find a few people you can rely on to give good help. I've been fortunate in that historically, most of my managers and a lot of my coworkers have been really helpful.
